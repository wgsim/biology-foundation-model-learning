01. Foundations — Tokens, Objectives, Evaluation

English translation. AI-assisted translation of the Korean original. The Korean original is authoritative and may be newer. Links to pages without an English version open the Korean original.

Duration: Weeks 1–2 (about 16–20 hours) · Compute tier: 🟢 local Mac (CPU/MPS) Prerequisites: Transformers and basic deep learning (intermediate), molecular-biology basics (intermediate). If you feel you are short on either, first do the self-assessment in 00_orientation.

Notation: † marks a statement that could not be confirmed from the abstract, the title or a search, and must be checked directly in the paper text. The cited papers are in sections 01–06 of the reading_list.

Learning goals¶

After this module you will be able to:

  1. Compare tokenization schemes for biological sequences (single residue, k-mer, BPE) and choose one to fit the task.

  2. Explain the equations and uses of self-supervised objectives (MLM, autoregressive, contrastive learning, diffusion).

  3. Explain the two ways of producing a zero-shot variant score with a pretrained model (masked marginal, log-likelihood ratio).

  4. Show by experiment how data leakage (homology, etc.) inflates performance, and design a correct split.

  5. Check the minimum requirements for baselines, metrics and statistics in FM evaluation with a checklist.

1. Biological sequences as tokens¶

Tokenization is the job of deciding the unit that corresponds to a “word” in a language model. Which scheme you choose changes the sequence length (= attention cost), the vocabulary size and the training signal.

Questions for choosing

  • What is the input length? (hundreds for proteins vs tens of kb to Mb for genomic regions)

  • Do you need precise variant (SNV) resolution?

  • Does the masking/prediction target clash with the token boundaries?

  • Is the compression rate a gain, or does it hurt biological interpretation?

Hands-on 01_tokenizers: Apply the three schemes to the same synthetic sequence and directly measure the token count, vocabulary size, frame-shift sensitivity and masking leakage.

2.2 Positional representation¶

There are absolute position embeddings, relative positions, rotary encoding (RoPE) and ALiBi. According to their abstracts, DNABERT-2 uses ALiBi and Flashzoi uses rotary position encoding. The key difference among these methods is whether they can extrapolate to longer contexts.

2.3 Biology-specific considerations¶

  • Directionality: The reading direction of DNA sequence information is not fixed. For that reason, bidirectional models or reverse-complement equivariance (Caduceus) are used.

  • Length distribution: Proteins and genomic regions differ in length by thousands of times, so the architecture choice diverges.

  • Supervised vs self-supervised: Enformer and Borzoi are trained with supervision to directly predict experimental tracks, while DNABERT, Evo and others are self-supervised and pretrained on sequence alone. They must not be ranked in the same table.

3.1 Zero-shot variant effect scores¶

Under the assumption that the pretraining distribution reflects evolutionary constraint, variants are scored without additional training.

  • Masked marginal (MLM): mask position iii and compute   s=log⁡p(xi=mut∣x∖i)−log⁡p(xi=wt∣x∖i)\;s=\log p(x_i=\text{mut}\mid x_{\setminus i}) - \log p(x_i=\text{wt}\mid x_{\setminus i})s=logp(xi​=mut∣x∖i​)−logp(xi​=wt∣x∖i​)

  • Whole-sequence log-likelihood ratio (autoregressive, etc.):   s=log⁡p(xmut)−log⁡p(xwt)\;s=\log p(x^{\text{mut}})-\log p(x^{\text{wt}})s=logp(xmut)−logp(xwt)

Multiple variants: The masked marginal above is for a single substitution. When there are several variants, MLM commonly assumes independence between positions and sums the per-position scores. In contrast, the autoregressive likelihood ratio uses the joint probability of the whole sequence. The two approaches therefore differ fundamentally in how they handle interactions between variants (epistasis). Do not conclude that one is better; compare them on a benchmark.

There is a caution on interpretation. This score is a proxy for “how plausible the variant is under the natural sequence distribution”. How well it agrees with actual fitness differs by assay and by protein family, so it must be validated with a benchmark (ProteinGym, etc.).

4. Scaling¶

  • The loss of language models follows a power law with model size, data and compute (Kaplan et al.). There is also a result that, for a given compute budget, the model and the number of tokens should grow together (Hoffmann et al.). Read the two papers and compare how their conclusions differ.

  • Biology examples (per the abstracts): the ESM-2 paper reported that growing the model up to 15B makes atomic-level structure information appear in the representations, and InterPLM reported that ESM-2 650M holds more interpretable concepts than 8M.

  • However, whether “bigger is always better” holds in biology depends on the task and the data. For variant-effect and single-cell tasks, check directly in the relevant benchmarks and critique papers the results of comparisons with small models and simple baselines (modules 02 and 04).

  • On the data side, sequence databases have large phylogenetic and species sampling bias and many duplicates. The results depend on the criterion used for deduplication (clustering).

5. Pitfalls of evaluation — the core of this module¶

5.1 Homology leakage¶

Sequences are evolutionarily related, so they are not independent samples (i.i.d.). With a random split, close relatives of the test sequences end up in the training set. The reported performance then becomes performance on “a new member of a known family”, not on “a new family”. The problem is that a high score can be obtained even if the model does not generalize and relies on memorization.

However, a random split is not always wrong. If the actual purpose of use is “predicting new variants/new members of known families”, a random split can also be the evaluation that fits that purpose. Three things matter: state the evaluation target (the deployment distribution), use the split that fits that target, and report the distribution of similarity between train and test. Differences in distribution between families and in label composition can also cause performance differences, so “memorization” is treated as only one of the candidate causes.

Hands-on 03_homology_split_leakage: With synthetic protein families, compare a random split vs a cluster/family split, and plot the accuracy by bin of maximum sequence identity to the training set.

The following is an MMseqs2 clustering example. After installing it, check the options with mmseqs easy-cluster -h.

A caution: clustering based on representative sequences does not link all pairs by single linkage, so pairs of sequences belonging to different clusters can also be at least as similar as the threshold. If strict separation is needed, search the train–test pairs directly and check the maximum identity. Also check the meaning of the coverage options (-c, --cov-mode) with mmseqs easy-cluster -h and report them together.

5.3 Baseline¶

When evaluating an FM, always report the following together:

  • Simple baselines: amino-acid/k-mer frequency, linear and logistic regression, mean prediction.

  • Comparison baselines (methods that are not large pretrained FMs): generative models trained per family with an MSA (EVE), CNN-based sequence-to-function models (DeepSEA), probabilistic models such as scVI, etc. These are the items tagged [기준선] (baseline) in the reading_list. EVE is an MSA-based model, not a “simple” model, so report it separately from the simple baselines.

  • Same conditions: the same split and the same tuning budget.

5.4 Metrics and statistics¶

  • Report rank correlation (Spearman), and for classification with imbalanced classes also report AUPRC.

  • Obtain confidence intervals by a group-level (family/donor) bootstrap. A sample-level bootstrap underestimates the uncertainty because the samples are correlated with each other.

  • Repeat with different seeds and report the variance. However, the standard deviation over repeated splits and the group-bootstrap confidence interval are different kinds of uncertainty. The former is the variation due to how the split is made, and the latter is the uncertainty that comes from sampling independent groups. When the number of groups is small (e.g., a few dozen families), both values are large and the confidence interval becomes wide. Report the two separately.

5.5 DOME recommendations (Walsh et al. 2021)¶

It organizes the validation recommendations for supervised ML into four areas: Data, Optimization, Model and Evaluation. Read the paper and map it onto the checklist in 5.6 (reading_list 01 ★).

5.6 Checklist¶

  • Did you set the split rule before looking at the data?

  • Did you measure the maximum sequence identity (or donor overlap) between train and test?

  • Did you check the overlap between the pretraining data and the evaluation data?

  • Did you run the simple baselines and the pre-FM methods on the same split?

  • Did you report group-level confidence intervals?

6. Comprehension check¶

Q1. In a non-overlapping 6-mer tokenizer, what happens to the tokens if 1 base is inserted at the very beginning of the sequence? What about a single-nucleotide tokenizer?

Answer
In non-overlapping k-mers, the window boundaries shift by 1 position, so almost every token changes into a different 6-mer in the vocabulary. In a single-nucleotide tokenizer, only 1 token is added at the very front, and all the others just shift position by 1. Check it numerically in notebook 01.

Q2. Why must you not mask only one token with MLM in overlapping 6-mers?

Answer
Because the neighboring tokens share bases with the masked token. The adjacent token shares 5 bases, so the bases of the masked span remain as they are in the neighboring tokens. Prediction then becomes easy and the training signal weakens. To hide a particular base, you must mask together, as a continuous span, the overlapping tokens that contain that base. To hide all 6 bases of one token, 2k−1=11 consecutive tokens are needed, and even then some of the bases at both edges remain in tokens outside the mask. Check this in section 6 of notebook 01.

Q3. What does it mean, and what does it not mean, that the masked-marginal score of an MLM-trained model is high?

Answer
It means that the variant is relatively plausible within the natural sequence distribution (evolutionary constraint). It does not guarantee that the variant preserves the function in a particular assay. The correlation differs by task and by protein, so it must be validated with a benchmark.

Q4. What does it mean when the performance gap between a random split and a cluster split is large?

Answer
It is a signal that the model (or the features) depends on similarity to the training families. Besides memorization, candidate causes include differences in distribution between families and differences in label composition. If you want to know performance on new families, random-split performance is an overestimate. On the other hand, if the goal is to predict new members of known families, it can be an appropriate evaluation for that goal. So state the evaluation goal.

Q5. What should you watch for when comparing the zero-shot scores of an autoregressive model and an MLM model in the same table?

Answer
The score definitions differ. One is a per-position masked marginal and the other is a whole-sequence log-likelihood ratio. The tokenizers, the pretraining data and the context lengths also differ. Compare them on the same evaluation data and metric (Spearman, etc.), but also report the differences in model size and data.

Q6. What problem arises if the evaluation split of a single-cell FM mixes the same donor into train and test?

Answer
The model memorizes donor-specific batch/genetic effects and the generalization performance is inflated. Performance on new donors and new studies is the real concern, so the split must be by donor/study.

Q7. Find in the two papers how Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. differ in how they allocate the compute budget between model size and data, and summarize it in one paragraph. (A paper-reading exercise with no answer.)

Q8. What procedure is needed to check the overlap between the pretraining corpus and the evaluation data?

Answer
Query the evaluation sequences against the pretraining corpus (if it is public) by sequence-identity search/clustering (e.g., MMseqs2), and report the distribution of the maximum identity. If the corpus is not public, state it as a limitation and consider evaluating on data released after the pretraining (a time split).
